SIPOC · finding the right process

Before you measure, you have to scope the process cleanly – otherwise you end up working on the wrong process. SIPOC (Supplier · Input · Process · Output · Customer) uses a bird's-eye view to find the steps where you suspect the causes of the problem (the big Y). The art is choosing the right altitude. When do I need a SIPOC – and what for, exactly?

The most common question in the course. Here is the answer, using concrete everyday situations.

SIPOC is not a diagram for the filing cabinet. It is the tool for the moment when you are asked to improve a process but don't yet know exactly what everyone is actually talking about. In 60–90 minutes it creates a shared map – before anyone starts measuring or solving.

You are given an improvement assignment “Onboarding takes far too long – please take care of it.” Where does the process start, where does it end? Who is involved? SIPOC clarifies the scope before you invest effort.
Two departments pass the buck “That's down to IT.” – “No, HR delivers too late.” Without a shared view of the process, people argue about responsibility instead of causes. SIPOC makes the handovers visible.
You take over someone else's process “From Monday, this is yours.” You know neither the suppliers nor the recipients. SIPOC is the fastest way to get up to speed – in a single picture.
Complaints are piling up “The bookings are constantly wrong.” The symptoms are known, the process behind them is not. SIPOC shows where measuring is possible at all.

✓ What SIPOC is for

  • Scope the process: where does it begin, where does it end?
  • Get everyone involved to a shared view
  • Make handovers and interfaces visible – that is where most problems arise
  • Find measurement points: which outputs can be counted?
  • Sharpen the assignment before time goes into data collection

✕ What SIPOC is not for

  • Not a detailed flowchart – 4 to 7 steps are enough
  • Not a root cause analysis – that comes afterwards (Ishikawa, data)
  • Not a collection of solutions – whoever solves here skips the understanding
  • Not a value stream – times and inventories do not belong in it
  • Not an organisation chart – it is about flow, not hierarchy
1 · TriggerA process should get better – the assignment is still vague.
2 · SIPOCScope it together: start, stop, steps, suppliers, customers, metrics.
3 · AgreementEveryone sees the same process. The scope is fixed in writing.
4 · MeasureCollect data on the output metrics you found (baseline).
5 · AnalyseOnly now look for causes – within the defined scope, using data.

In the Six Sigma sequence, SIPOC sits in the Define phase – before Measure. It is the first thing you do and rarely takes longer than half a day.

SIPOC at a glance

Five columns, one process in the middle. You read from the outside in – but fill it in from the inside out.

SSupplier

Who provides the inputs? A department, a system, a person.

IInput

What flows into the process and is consumed or processed?

PProcess

The scoped steps – 4 to 7 – in which you suspect the causes.

OOutput

What comes out? Including by-products: documentation, feedback, metrics.

CCustomer

Who receives the outputs? Internal recipients count too.

The goal is not a perfect flowchart but to scope the overall process in which the causes of the big Y lie. Every output needs a customer, every input needs a supplier. The idea behind it: Y = f(x) – the result is a function of the process steps.

Reflection & learning transfer

The real learning work – after filling it in.

1 · Where was the altitude decision hardest?

Most teams change the granularity at least once between phase 2 and phase 4. Was that the case for you too – and what prompted it: a team statement, choosing the scope, or defining the inputs/outputs?

2 · Which team statement revealed the most?

At least three statements point to causes outside your scope (e.g. code review or hardware procurement). Which ones were they – and do you actually need two or three parallel SIPOCs, or is one with clearly marked interfaces enough?

3 · How would the SIPOC change if the Y were different?

Suppose the real problem were not time but satisfaction after 90 days: which steps would drop out, which would be added? The exercise shows how closely the Y and the process are linked.
